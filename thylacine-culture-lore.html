Explore how the thylacine shaped culture and inspired lore

Unlike many extinct animals, the thylacine lived alongside humans into modern times. It was seen, hunted, filmed, and remembered. As it disappeared, it moved from landscape into story, becoming part of folklore and ongoing speculation.

[ Thylacine Ancient Lore ]

A Shadow across landscape and story

Aboriginal Australian and Tasmanian Aboriginal Peoples

For Aboriginal Australians, the thylacine was not a mythical creature but part of their living world. It was known, observed, and understood within local ecosystems long before written records. Its presence is reflected in rock art and oral traditions, particularly in northern Australia, where striped, dog-like figures are widely interpreted as thylacines.

Ancient Lore / Rock Art Overview

Thylacine depiction in rock art

Thylacine-like figures appear in ancient rock art across northern Australia, including Arnhem Land and the Kimberley. These images often show a long-bodied animal with a narrow head and distinctive back markings. Some are believed to date back thousands of years, offering visual evidence of the species’ former presence on the mainland.

Known by
Many Names

The thylacine was known by different names across Indigenous languages, particularly among Tasmanian Aboriginal peoples. Early records document names such as “coorinna,” “loarinna,” and “laoonana” (also spelled loinana or luanina), reflecting regional languages and colonial-era transcription.

Because many Tasmanian Aboriginal languages were disrupted following colonization, these names survive mainly through early word lists recorded by European observers. They represent fragments of a much broader linguistic and cultural understanding of the animal.
