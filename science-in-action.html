[ Sequencing the ASIAN ELEPHANT Genome ]
CLSL-LABS CLSL-LABS

More that 50 woolly mammoth genomes have been assembled by our academic collaborators. With sequencing coverage averaging 11× and exceeding 30× in the best samples, these genomes span thousands of years and capture mammoth evolution in detail. Asian elephants share 99.96% of their DNA with mammoths, making them the closest living relative. Genomes from 23 Asian elephants are sequenced at high depth, including a publicly available reference genome that we sequenced in collaboration with the VGP. With our collaborators at UConn, we've also generated the first complete telomere-to-telomere assembly for the Asian elephant, enabling precise mammoth–elephant comparisons.

[ Identifying Cold-Adapted Traits ]
CLSL-LABS CLSL-LABS

Our comparative genomic analysis identified nearly 1.5 million mammoth-specific variants. By focusing on variants that are present in all mammoths but absent in elephants, and then prioritizing those variants that change gene regulation and function, our team can pinpoint the DNA sequence changes that underlie mammoth traits such as long and dense hair, fat storage, and cold-adaptation.

[ Deriving Cells and Designing Edits ]
CLSL-LABS CLSL-LABS

Elephant skin and hair follicle samples are used to derive living cells, including dermal papilla cells capable of forming hair-like structures. In parallel, our stem cell team derived the first Asian elephant induced pluripotent stem cells, which can be transformed into other cell types for testing hypotheses about how mammoth-specific DNA variants change how genes behave and, eventually, the animal's phenotype.

"TAD= Topologically Associated Domain"

Reconstructing Traits Through 3D Genome Architecture

///////////////////////////////////////////////////

+

We developed a pipeline that integrates transcription factor binding site (TFBS) analysis with 3D chromatin architecture, specifically topologically associating domains (TADs), to investigate how mammoth genetic variants influence gene regulation through chromatin reorganization. Our computational analysis identifies mammoth-specific variants that alter CTCF binding sites. These changes can create or disrupt TAD boundaries, either forming new regulatory neighborhoods or merging previously separate ones. As a result, a small number of mammoth-specific variants may have substantial phenotypic effects by reshaping TAD structure and altering the regulation of many genes.

+

TADs are a structural unit of the genome that organize DNA into self-interacting regions that allow Enhancers, Promoters, and regulatory elements to interact efficiently.  TADs are formed by cohesion motor proteins that reel in DNA to create loops, growing until they encounter molecular "stop signs" called CTCF proteins. These proteins lock the DNA into organized, self-contained neighborhoods. A TAD can span hundreds of thousands to millions of base pairs, so that even if a gene and enhancer are far apart on the genome, the 3D loop structure can bring them close so they can interact to regulate gene expression. 

TAD-Based Regulatory Genomics Pipeline The pipeline integrates transcription factor binding site (TFBS) analysis with sequence-based prediction of TAD boundaries to identify regulatory changes:

Mammoth-Specific Regulatory Rewiring

Colossal used this approach to find mammoth-specific variants that alter CTCF binding and reorganize TAD boundaries. These changes can create, remove, or merge regulatory neighborhoods, reshaping enhancer–gene interactions.

Because a single TAD can regulate many genes, a small number of variants can drive large phenotypic effects, influencing complex traits such as body size, ear length, tail length, and hair growth. This work represents a shift from single-gene analysis to systems-level engineering of extinct traits.

FGF5

The FGF5 gene is a signalling protein that acts as a regulator for hair growth.

+

FGF5 is expressed toward the end of the anagen phase (growth). FGF5 expression instructs the hair follicle to transition to the catagen phase (regression).

+

Reduced expression of FGF5 causes the hair follicle growth phase to last longer, resulting in longer hair. This has been demonstrated in mice, cats, dogs, cows. and several other species.

+

One of the modifications engineered into the genomes of our Colossal Woolly Mice was an edit that disrupted the expression of FGF5, which contributed to their longer hair.

We identified mammoth-specific variants in CTCF binding sites that result in the gain of a TAD that elephants do not have. The HOXB13 gene is within this mammoth-specific TAD.

We hypothesize that this difference in 3D genomic architecture (the TAD) alters HOXB13 gene expression. If correct, this DNA variant in the mammoth genome may contribute to the mammoth’s longer tail compared to the Asian elephant.

We are designing and conducting experiments to test whether elephant cells engineered with the mammoth version of the CTCF binding site show different HOXB13 expression compared to cells with the elephant version.

[ INtroducing the CLSL WOolly Mouse ]

The Colossal Woolly Mouse: First Living Proof of Mammoth Traits

To validate that mammoth genes can function in living systems, Colossal created the Colossal Woolly Mouse. Using a combination of CRISPR-Cas9 knockouts, precision editing, and homology-directed repair, our scientists engineered mice with as many as 11 edits across 7 different genes. These mice express mammoth-inspired traits including longer hair, woolly coat texture, altered lipid metabolism, and a distinctive coat coloration.

The Colossal Woolly Mouse proves that mammoth-inspired traits can be revived in a living organism. The Colossal Woolly Mice are an important scientific milestone—and some of the cutest coworkers imaginable—and confirm that complex extinct traits can be engineered safely and predictably.
